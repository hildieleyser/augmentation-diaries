Augmentation Diaries

Human senses are being rebuilt, replaced and added to. What that is actually like remains close to undocumented.

Conversations with the engineers and neuroscientists doing it, the clinicians who send people home with it, and the people living with the result.

About the podcast

Augmentation Diaries is about what happens to people after a device is fitted, and about the distance between what a technology is meant to do and what it turns out to be like to live with.

The shape of the series is still open. What exists now is the research: hour-long conversations with the people building this technology, the clinicians who send people home with it, and the people who use it daily, all transcribed in full. The work almost always begins with a stated goal, usually restoration or rehabilitation, and what we ask about is everything that follows from it. The technical detail interests us for its own sake as well, and these conversations go a long way into it.

What keeps coming up

One

What the device actually delivers

A retinal implant sends dots of light to the brain and leaves the person wearing it to work out what the dots mean. Touch through an electrode array arrives the same way. Hardly anyone gets back the sense they lost, and there is still no agreed word for the one they get instead.

Two

The second effect

A leg prosthesis gets someone walking, and then the asymmetry goes to work on the healthy side. One engineer described people who end up in a wheelchair twenty years later because of what the compensation did to the good hip, rather than because of the amputation.

Three

Tested in the wrong room

A brain in a soundproof booth is not doing what a brain does on patrol. One lab runs its whole protocol twice: once in the quiet room, then again on a treadmill in front of a screen that wraps 180 degrees, with radio chatter on the speakers and a rifle weighted and recoiling like the real one.

And underneath most of it

What gets funded and what gets asked for

Machine learning for prosthetic control funds easily. A better liner for the socket does not, though the man running 5k has to stop and drain his sleeve. One engineer has watched ideas win money for sounding futuristic while the thing his participants kept raising went unfunded.

Nobody talks about the life afterwards

A surgical consultation covers the level of the cut and what happens to the nerves. The patient wants to know what next year looks like. One rehabilitation director argues that a rehabilitator and a psychologist belong in that room alongside the surgeon.

The company can change its mind

Funding, hardware and access sit with manufacturers who reorganise, swap chief executives and drop a research direction. Senior engineers say the work is promising and that their hands are tied.

Whether it reads as augmentation at all

A device that looks futuristic is an asset in London and a stigma in Rwanda, where a natural-looking limb is the one that gets worn. And a lot of people are not after a new ability. They have a limb that half works, and nothing exists between a prosthesis and a brace.

People trade the damage knowingly

Rowers with terrible technique keep it because it wins medals. A man was told for six months that his new leg would never take a fall, then went skiing in the first week of the season. The trade is usually made with the information in hand.

Some of it cannot be put into words

Thirty years of hypotheses about phantom limb pain have not explained it, so nobody can treat it properly either. As one neuroscientist put it, the hard part is that people often cannot describe themselves.

Who is making it

Editorial

Gregory Warner

Gregory created and hosted Rough Translation at NPR, where he spent a decade building narrative series, among them a seven-part run on the distance between civilians and the military in the United States. Before that he was NPR's East Africa correspondent and reported from Afghanistan and Pakistan.

He leads the editorial side of the project and conducts most of the interviews.

Research

Hildie Leyser

Hildie works on multisensory integration, mostly olfaction, and is Head of Research at Netholabs, where she builds olfactory wearables. On this project she runs the literature, the interview protocol and the record of first-person descriptions that the series is built on.

The Plasticity Lab

The project is hosted by the Plasticity Lab, Tamar Makin’s group, which studies what changes in the brain after a hand is lost or an extra one is added. The lab runs studies on prosthesis use, phantom sensation and cortical representation after amputation, and Dani Clode’s Third Thumb was designed there, so a good deal of the work the series covers is going on in the next room.

The affiliation reaches the reporting as well. The lab’s most argued-over paper makes the case that the field has overstated how much the brain reorganises itself after injury, and that same scepticism runs through the series.

How the research interviews go

An hour, and rarely only one

The first conversation is recorded and transcribed in full, and most of them run over. People rarely describe an experience well the first time they are asked, so we go back.

The exact phrases get kept

There is no agreed vocabulary for any of this, so we keep a record of how people put it, in their words rather than ours. That record has been the most useful thing in the research so far.

We follow the person into the technical detail

Interviews start with how someone ended up in this field, and the engineering arrives when they bring it. Nothing gets simplified on the way out.
